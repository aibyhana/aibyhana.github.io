What a Stanford Ethics Course Taught Me About Building Better AI

Created on November 08, 2025

2025   ·   ethics   ai-policy   augmentation   ·   ai-safety

Before attending Stanford’s Ethics, Technology, and Public Policy course, I believed I understood generative AI’s promise. Working in Gen AI development, I deploy these systems for enterprises. Yet the course fundamentally shifted my perspective on the ethical weight of technological choices.

The Central Metaphor: Omelas

The course opened with Ursula K. Le Guin’s philosophical thought experiment “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas,” depicting a utopian city whose prosperity depends entirely on one child’s suffering. Rather than asking whether people should abandon such systems, I realized the crucial question is: “What about the ones who stay and fight to rebuild the city on different terms?”

This reframing became my lens for examining AI development ethics.

Three Major Insights

1. Fairness as Value Encoding

Studying the COMPAS recidivism prediction algorithm revealed a crucial truth: you generally can’t achieve multiple definitions of fairness simultaneously. The algorithm was simultaneously fair and unfair depending on metrics used. I recognized that “bias mitigation” isn’t merely technical—it’s inherently ethical, encoding decisions about whose suffering we accept and whose efficiency we prioritize.

2. The Automation vs. Augmentation Choice

Drawing on economist Daron Acemoglu’s framework, I identified two AI development paths:

  • Full Automation: AI replaces human workers, leading to inequality
  • Augmentation: AI enhances human decision-making and creates new capabilities

The consequences of any technology depend on who gets to make pivotal decisions about how the technology develops. Currently, a small tech elite optimizes for cost reduction.

3. The Intimacy Economy

Generative AI introduces new surveillance risks. Unlike social media competing for attention, conversational AI competes for intimacy. When users share sensitive concerns with AI systems designed to extract data for advertising, manipulation becomes qualitatively different—an infiltration of trust rather than mere attention interruption.

Additionally, an MIT study found 25% of highest-quality training data has been restricted via robots.txt—what researchers call an “emerging crisis in consent.”

Concrete Commitments Forward

Next 30 Days: Audit current projects through an ethical lens, documenting who benefits and who bears costs of automation.

Next Quarter: Shift internal conversations from “How can we automate?” to “How can we augment human capability?”

This Year: Deepen knowledge of human-complementary AI research and advocate for open-source alternatives, enabling local deployment and organizational data control.

The Broader Stakes

We have a 5-10 year window before AI’s trajectory solidifies. I call for:

  • Building with workers, not just tech leadership
  • Measuring capability enhancement over headcount reduction
  • Demanding transparency about training data and deployment impacts
  • Choosing augmentation over replacement where possible
  • Amplifying worker voice in technology governance

If AI brings a dystopian future, as Acemoglu and Johnson note, it will be because we failed to grasp how this technology could help workers rather than replace them.

Call to Collaboration

I share this publicly because individual intentions prove insufficient. I invite others developing AI systems to discuss automation-versus-augmentation choices and collaborate on ethical frameworks—recognizing that meaningful change requires coordinated pressure across technologists, workers, managers, policymakers, and civil society organizations.
